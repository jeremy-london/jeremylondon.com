I liked Astro 4 because it improved the maintenance loop around content sites without trying to turn every site into a miniature web app.

The Dev Toolbar made the boundary visible in the browser. A page can look static while carrying a few interactive islands, and terminal output is not always the place I am looking when I am checking the page. Seeing diagnostics alongside the page makes small problems easier to catch: too much JavaScript, a missing image description, an island that loads when it should not.

That sounds like a convenience feature. It changes behavior because it lowers the cost of noticing a mistake.

The same applies to routing. A blog has an archive, tags, feeds, old links, and content that continues representing the author long after publication. A docs site has ordering, language choices, and search. "Static" describes the delivery model, not the absence of product decisions.

Astro 4's work around content and internationalization made those decisions more explicit. Locale support means paths, fallback behavior, links, and selection rules. Most personal sites will not need every option, but the framework is right to treat the problem as real when it appears.

Build time also affects editorial quality. When a site takes too long to rebuild, people skip the full check. They change something else while waiting, or decide that the last successful build is good enough. Caching content work gives the boring checks a chance to run again: schemas, routes, images, feeds, and imports.

View transitions are useful for a similar reason. They can help a reader keep their place during navigation without making the entire site behave like a client-rendered application. The page remains the unit of meaning; the transition just reduces the abruptness of moving between pages.

That is what I remember from Astro 4. The release did not make content sites bigger for the sake of being bigger. It made the surrounding work feel less like a collection of exceptions.
