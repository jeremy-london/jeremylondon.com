The useful detail in Anthropic's [Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2) is that they are the same underlying model with different safeguards and availability.

Fable is the broadly available configuration. The announcement says it includes safeguards that block high-risk cybersecurity and biology tasks. Mythos has fewer of those restrictions, and access is limited to vetted organizations working in those areas. Anthropic also describes model-specific evaluations and monitoring as part of the release process.

That makes the model name only part of the deployment decision. The capability, the policy layer around it, and the account allowed to use it form one package. Two teams can call a model from the same family and have meaningfully different action space.

This is a useful way to think about model governance because access controls can be concrete. Instead of relying on a team to remember which prompts are sensitive, the service can scope access to an organization, apply a distinct configuration, and log which route handled a request.

The remaining question is how much of that boundary is visible to the customer. If a model silently falls back, blocks a request, or routes a task to another configuration, the application needs a record of that decision. Otherwise, an incident review or quality regression will blame "the model" without identifying which model behavior and safeguards were actually in play.

For teams adopting models with different risk profiles, I would track at least the model configuration, account or program eligibility, policy decision, and any fallback in the request record. The access policy is part of the model contract.
